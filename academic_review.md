Previous studies have demonstrated that breast-cancer diagnosis is an active and rapidly developing research area. Diagnosis (CADx) differs from detection (CADe). The latter evaluates whether a the imagery displays a tumour while the former assesses whether it is benign or malignant (1). The study at hand focuses on diagnosis only (CADx). Both CADe and CADx require a reliable dataset for training. Such data are scarce. Varoquaux, G. and Cheplygina, V emphasizes that available datasets suffer from several biases. Although it is a common practise in machine learning to build a test set that is a subset of the same data pool as the train set, there is a risk of validating a model's performance on a population that does not reflect the distribution of the actual population, incurring poor performance of the model in real-life situations. In addition, a dataset may not necessarily capture the full extent of medical conditions, leading to the model's underperformance if it was trained on data underestimating them. The dataset also requires high-quality and curated images. Indeed, they can sometimes be polluted with the trace of medical intervention, leading the model to learn inappropriate features. Besides, the labelling needs to be accurate and of high quality. Finally, the set should not contain any patient leakage because it can overestimate the obtained result by having the model learning patterns that will be found in the test set (2). 

Being aware of these shortcomings, this study takes the CBIS-DDSM reference dataset. It is a curated version of the DDSM dataset, which is a collection of mammograms from different sources, "Massachusetts General Hospital, Wake Forest University School of Medicine, Sacred Heart Hospital, and Washington University of St Louis School of Medicine" (1). To some extent, the CBIS-DDSM dataset represents an attempt to tackle the biases the legacy DDSM faced as decribed by Lee, R., Gimenez, F., Hoogi, A. et al. First, the accessibility of data is improved. All the metadata is gathered in a single csv file, including patients' specificities (BI-RADS, age, etc). Some mass cases were also questionable as to whether they were actual sign of disease. With the help of a trained mammographer, 339 images were removed that could not be appropriately confirmed. Considering the original DDSM dataset accounted for 2620 scanned film mammography studies, this cut accounts for a sizeable portion at risk of orienting the result of a training. The CBIS-DDSM dataset also comes with an enhanced region-of-interest (ROI) segmentation for mass cases. The original DDSM was providing a mere location of the mass tumour, which is not sufficient for a model to properly train. By using the original ROI as a rough starting point, the region was further cropped to the exact contour using Chan and Vese model (3). Finally, the CBIS-DDSM also comes up with a standardized train/test split. For mass cases, which this studies focuses on, the train set accounts for 51.37% benign cases and 48.63% malignant cases. The test set, on the other hand, is 58.5% benign and 41.5% malignant.

As previously stated, the CBIS-DDSM is a bank of medical cases. A case indicates a certain abnormality. The medical process entails two mammograms per abnormality, the craniocaudal (CC) and the mediolateral oblique (MLO) views. The CBIS-DDSM dataset also comes with a cropped image of the region and a mask for the full image. Every case should thus come up with four documents. There is thus plenty of pre-processing options. Liao, L., Aagaard, E.M. first converts all DICOMs into .png. The authors built a pipeline that consists in applying the mask to the full mammogram and that centers around the tumour. The mask is strict, meaning that pixels outside the region of interest are set to black. Ruling out the default cropped images allows the authors to control the preprocessing pipeline rather than relying directly on the provided cropped images. As a prerequisite, the mask format should match the mammogram's. If not, the case is ruled out because it is not considered reliable. The main limitation of a strict ROI-masking approach is that it may remove contextual information surrounding the lesion.

Abdikenov B, Zhaksylyk T, Imasheva A, Orazayev Y, Karibekov T. highlights that tumour diagnosis occurs in a contextual analysis of the full mammogram. The presence of calcifications - compatible with a malignant form - the breast density, whether it is a CC or a MLO view orient the mammographer's judgment. These information can only be appreciated by assessing the full mammogram, not just the abnormality. Their study concludes that training two models, one on CC views and the other one on MLO views, and, then, merging their features, gives better results than training a model on a merged image of both CC and MLO views. As such, it emphasizes the importance of the context. The initial can somehow perform well under some models, like VGG19 (accuracy : 0.8865 and F1 : 0.6783) or DenseNet (accuracy : 0.7152 and F1 : 0.7582), but the merge blurs the signal and prevent generalization. According to the authors' findings, two distinct models generalize better on heterogeneous data when trained on well-defined views. Under this set up, the DenseNet model achieves an accuracy of 0.717 and an F1-score of 0.7756.

The approach does not ponder any masking though, meaning the tumour assessment is achieved in isolation of a focus on the abnormality. The mammogram is considered as a full input and, as such, there is no notion of region of interest. A full-image or multi-view approach may capture contextual information, but it may also make it harder to isolate the contribution of the lesion itself because the patterns are learnt from an image containing a lot more information than the presence of a mass. There is a risk that the model learns global correlation of malignancy rather than lesion-specific features. That is why Yiqiu Shen, et al. (6) adopts a hybrid approach combining a full mammogram view and ROI study called GMIC. Although the model trained at the full mammogram level has a CADe purpose (detection of an abnormality), its features are merged to the other model's, encouraging the CADx process to acknoledge the full context. This study generates performance comparable to radiologists under the conditions evaluated while consuming fewer memory of GPU and being 4.3 times faster than ResNet-34.

Varoquaux and Cheplygina stress that medical imaging studies require meaningful baselines, because performance claims are difficult to interpret if the model is not compared against an appropriate reference (2). This is particularly relevant here, since the project does not only aim to train a CNN, but to compare different representations of the same lesion. A baseline is therefore needed to determine whether masking, contextual cropping, or global-local inputs actually improve performance. Chollet’s deep learning workflow provides a practical framework for implementing a binary classification model. It consists in beginning with a simple working model, monitor validation performance, diagnose overfitting or underfitting, and only then increase model complexity.

Baseline selection is not the only methodological issue; metric choice also affects how model performance is interpreted. It is not rare that accuracy is used as a primary metric to assess the model's performance. Abdikenov, et al. puts the metric forward when presenting a model. For instance, their study emphasizes that "the accuracy rose to 88.53%, with a remarkably high ROC AUC of 0.9283. These results suggest that the DBE architecture may better exploit inter-view feature complementarities or capture latent patterns in datasets." Although it is an interesting metric, the risk of false negatives does not yield the same risk as false positives in the medical profession. As a result, Varoquaux and Cheplygina insist that "important metrics may be missing from evaluation. Next to typical classification metrics (sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve), several authors argue for a calibration metric that compares the predicted and observed probabilities".

For this reason, this project should not rely on accuracy alone. AUC is useful for threshold-independent comparison, while recall/sensitivity is particularly important because false negatives are more problematic in a diagnostic setting. Precision remains relevant because excessive false positives may reduce clinical usefulness.

Overall, the reviewed literature suggests that CNN performance in mammographic CADx depends not only on architecture, but also on dataset quality, leakage control, lesion representation, contextual information, baseline choice, and metric selection.
